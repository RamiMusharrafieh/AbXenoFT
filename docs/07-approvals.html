The Human Antibody Gap
190 approved antibodies · 1994–2025

Antibodies got human. Animal models didn't.

Therapeutic antibodies have marched steadily away from mouse sequence: murine, then chimeric, then humanized, then fully human. In the last six years alone 34 fully human antibodies were approved worldwide, more than the entire field produced in its first two decades.

That progress creates a testing problem. A fully human antibody dosed into an immunocompetent mouse or macaque is foreign protein, and the animal raises anti-drug antibodies against its framework. The species-conditioned framework models built in this project convert a human framework to a mouse or macaque one while leaving the CDRs untouched, producing a species-matched surrogate for in vivo work.

The worked example is the first one

Adalimumab is the earliest fully human antibody in this dataset, approved 2002 and still among the best-selling drugs ever made. Running its VH through the two framework models gives a mouse and a macaque surrogate. CDRs are preserved verbatim; only framework residues change.

What the tool does and does not fix. Converting the framework addresses immunogenicity against the framework, the anti-drug antibody response that limits repeat dosing of a human mAb in an immunocompetent animal. It does not confer binding to a rodent ortholog of the target: the CDRs are deliberately unchanged, so specificity is unchanged. Where a human antibody simply does not recognise the mouse antigen, a framework-converted surrogate will not either, and a true surrogate with different CDRs is still required. Macaque targets are usually close enough to human that cross-reactivity holds, which is why macaque conversion is the more directly useful of the two.
